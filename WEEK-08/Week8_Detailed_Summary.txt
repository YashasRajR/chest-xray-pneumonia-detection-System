My eighth and final week at the company was entirely dedicated to wrapping up the project, testing it with real people, and making sure all my documentation was absolutely perfect. Since the backend code was completely stable and all the major bugs from the previous week's destructive testing were fixed, I completely stopped writing new features. It is very tempting to keep adding bells and whistles to a project, but I knew that handing over a clean, finished product was much more important than leaving behind a messy, half-finished one. My biggest priority was making sure that the engineering team at Akshar AI could easily take over my work on the pneumonia detection system after my internship ended and I went back to university.

To ensure a smooth handover, I had to perform what is known as a code freeze. Over the course of the two-month project, I had installed many different Python tools and libraries on my computer to make the software work, primarily TensorFlow for the machine learning models and Flask for the web server. Technology changes extremely fast, and these tools are constantly being updated by their creators. If someone else tried to run my code a year from now, and those tools had updated in the background, my code might completely break because the new tools might not be compatible with the old code. To prevent this nightmare scenario, I ran a command that scanned my computer and locked the exact, specific versions of every single tool I used into a single text file called a requirements file. Now, whenever the next developer takes over, they can install exactly what I used with one single command, guaranteeing the system will run perfectly forever, exactly as I left it.

After locking down the code and ensuring the technical environment was safe, I moved on to user testing. Up until this point, only engineers and mentors had looked at the medical website. I wanted to know if regular people could use it. I grabbed a few of my coworkers from different departments, like marketing and human resources, who had absolutely no idea how the project worked. I asked them to sit down at my computer and told them to try and use the medical dashboard to diagnose an X-ray. I did not give them any instructions on purpose. I just watched them try to navigate the interface to see if it was confusing. Watching them was incredibly helpful and rewarding. They were able to figure out the drag-and-drop file upload instantly because I had made the upload box large and prominent during my UI testing the previous week. They also immediately understood that a green result meant the patient was healthy and a red result meant the patient had pneumonia. Their positive feedback confirmed that my simple, clean, minimalist design choices from week two were correct, and that a good user interface doesn't need an instruction manual to operate.

The last few days of the week were the most writing-intensive part of the entire summer. I was required by the university and the company to submit a final thesis report detailing everything I had accomplished. I spent hours organizing my notes, reviewing my old code, and writing down my thought processes. I ended up writing a massive, fifty-page document. I broke it down into ten distinct chapters, covering everything from the early days of researching lung X-rays and gathering data, to the complex math behind shrinking the images, to the architecture of the web dashboard. I made sure to explain not just what I did, but why I did it, so that anyone reading it could learn from my mistakes and successes. I even added a professional cover page and styled the entire document with the company's signature colors to make it look like a published paper. 

Once the fifty-page report was finally finished, proofread, and formatted, I delivered a final presentation to my mentors, the engineering team, and the company leadership. I walked them through the timeline and showed them live demonstrations of the pneumonia detection dashboard, highlighting how fast and accurate the deep learning model was. After the presentation was well received and I answered all their questions, I gathered all my code, documentation, and the final report. I pushed all the files and folders to the company's main Git repository, double-checked that everything was uploaded correctly, and officially handed the completed project over to my mentors, marking a highly successful end to my summer internship.
